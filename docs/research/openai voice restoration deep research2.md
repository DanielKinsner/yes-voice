# Offline Voice Restoration for a Premium Vocal Polish App

## Executive direction

If the brief is **highest possible quality on already-recorded material, fully offline, no cloud dependency**, the smartest product direction is **not pure DSP-only** and **not plugin-first**. The current frontier for hard restoration problems—heavy broadband noise, intrusive room reverb, clipped or bandwidth-limited speech, and difficult singing-vocal cleanup—has moved toward **hybrid systems**: deterministic DSP for transparent surgical cleanup and control, combined with **local ML inference** for the cases where classic inverse filtering and masking hit a ceiling. Recent benchmark and challenge results point in that direction: the EARS benchmark reported that a generative method was preferred in listening tests for speech enhancement, the 2025 URGENT challenge was explicitly built around broader “universal” enhancement across seven distortion types, and newer systems such as AnyEnhance and VoiceRestore target denoising, dereverberation, declipping, super-resolution, and broader restoration in one framework. citeturn21view4turn24view0turn21view0turn22view0

For a commercial product, the best differentiator is therefore **a hybrid offline restoration workstation**: spectral editing plus batch rendering plus mode-aware restoration chains for **voiceover**, **isolated singing**, and **vocals inside mixed music**. That product shape also matches the way the strongest commercial tools are positioned today. iZotope RX 12 is still framed as an all-in-one restoration editor with spectrogram-based surgery, dedicated modules for clicks, clips, hum, reverb, noise, and stem-related workflows, while SpectraLayers Pro 12 emphasizes AI-assisted noisy-speech unmixing and voice-focused processing. That is strong evidence that a premium app should feel like a **restoration environment**, not merely a rack of one-click processors. citeturn40view0turn11search5turn11search13

My recommendation, based on the current state of the field, is this: **ship a standalone desktop app first, built around offline spectral restoration and multi-pass rendering; use DSP wherever it is still best-in-class; add local ML where it clearly beats DSP; postpone DAW embedding until the core quality bar is nailed.** ARA exists precisely because sophisticated audio processing benefits from random access to whole clips rather than being constrained by realtime buffers, which aligns very well with your “quality over realtime” requirement. citeturn18view11

## Where the frontier stands now

The speech-restoration world has shifted away from narrow one-task denoisers toward **universal enhancement**. The 2025 URGENT challenge defined the target explicitly: one enhancement system that can handle additive noise, reverberation, clipping, bandwidth extension, codec artifacts, packet loss, and wind noise, across multiple languages and sample rates. A 2025 challenge paper based on USEMamba reported that **regression-style enhancement worked well for most distortions**, but **generative modeling helped more on missing-content tasks** such as packet loss and bandwidth extension. That distinction matters for your app: “remove noise” and “reconstruct what isn’t there anymore” are not the same problem. citeturn24view0turn27view0

Speech and singing should also be treated as **different restoration domains**, even if the UI sells them under one roof. MBTFNet’s authors argue directly that conventional neural speech enhancement is not optimal for singing, and that generic music source-separation models can underperform when the real target is **vocal enhancement**, not simple stem isolation. Their model specifically targets removal of background music, noise, and even backing vocals from singing recordings. Then, in 2025, the SingVERSE benchmark made the same lesson harder to ignore: it found a consistent trade-off between perceptual quality and intelligibility across current singing-enhancement models, and showed that **training with in-domain singing data improves enhancement without degrading speech capability**. In plain English: a serious product should not run the same “voice cleaner” model on narration, acapella, and lead vocals buried in a mix and expect world-class results. citeturn33view0turn21view2turn21view3

Dereverberation is another place where the frontier has moved. For speech, classical WPE remains a respected baseline and is still important engineeringly. But for blind single-channel dereverb, newer generative methods such as **BUDDy** use diffusion priors to jointly estimate room acoustics and restore the dry signal, and the paper explicitly reports adaptability to **high-resolution singing voice dereverberation**. In parallel, earlier work on unsupervised vocal dereverb for music argued that artificial reverb is more diverse than natural room reverb and showed that diffusion-based approaches can outperform prior vocal dereverberation baselines. By 2025, ReverbFX and SingingReverbFX appeared specifically to benchmark **singing dereverberation under artificial reverb conditions**, which is extremely relevant for music production and post-created vocal ambience. citeturn18view5turn34view0turn22view8turn33view1

The commercial market reflects the same split. Acon’s DeVerberate 3 uses deep learning for automatic speech/vocal reverb reduction. Waves Clarity Vx Pro is positioned as AI voice noise reduction with real-time operation and multiband control. Accentize has pushed even farther into decomposition-style post tools: **dxSplit** is marketed as separating voice, reverb, and noise, while **dxRevive Pro** is framed as studio-quality speech restoration with local processing. These are not cloud utilities. They are desktop-native examples showing that **offline-capable, local ML audio cleanup is already accepted in the pro market**. citeturn18view8turn18view10turn18view9

## DSP foundations that still matter

You said you wanted the DSP math if possible, and that is absolutely the right instinct, because even in a modern hybrid app, DSP should remain the backbone for **predictability, explainability, and editability**.

For denoising, the canonical single-channel model in the STFT domain is

\[
Z_{t,k} = Y_{t,k} + W_{t,k},
\]

with noisy speech \(Z_{t,k}\), clean speech \(Y_{t,k}\), and noise \(W_{t,k}\). The simplest enhancement family is then

\[
\hat{Y}_{t,k} = G_{t,k} Z_{t,k},
\]

where the whole game is choosing the gain \(G_{t,k}\). In the classic Gaussian model, the Wiener estimator becomes the MMSE estimator of the spectral components,

\[
\hat{Y}_t = \left[\Lambda_{Y_t}(\Lambda_{Y_t}+\Lambda_{W_t})^{-1}\right] Z_t,
\]

which is the frequency-domain template behind the familiar Wiener gain \(G=\xi/(\xi+1)\) with \(\xi\) the a priori SNR. The same review also lays out the a priori and a posteriori SNR definitions \(\xi_{t,k}\) and \(\gamma_{t,k}\), plus the Ephraim–Malah gain for MMSE log-spectral amplitude estimation:

\[
G(\xi,\gamma)=\frac{\xi}{\xi+1}\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\vartheta}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-x}}{x}\,dx\right),
\quad
\vartheta=\frac{\xi}{\xi+1}\gamma.
\]

That is still important in 2026 because it gives you a highly controllable baseline, especially on moderate noise, and it remains a strong building block for residual cleanup and “natural mode” rendering. citeturn30view0turn38view0

That same literature also explains why **spectral floors** matter. In practice, purely subtractive methods create musical-noise artifacts when bins are over-attenuated, so practical systems impose a floor instead of allowing magnitude estimates to collapse. Cohen’s review links this directly to the “spectral floor” modification of spectral subtraction. That design idea is old, but for a premium app it is still one of the easiest ways to expose **Natural / Balanced / Aggressive** cleanup modes without redesigning the whole engine. citeturn38view0

For dereverberation, WPE remains the key classical model worth implementing. The STFT-domain reverberant observation can be written as a late-reverb prediction problem, where the desired dereverberated signal \(d_{n,k}\) is estimated from the current frame minus a delayed linear prediction built from past frames. In the MCLP/WPE formulation summarized by Parchami et al., the late reverb is modeled through prediction vectors \(G_k\) and past-frame stacks \(X_{n,k}\), yielding

\[
d_{n,k}=x^{(1)}_{n,k}-G_k^{H}X_{n,k}.
\]

The practical point is not the exact notation; it is the modeling choice: **late reverb is predictable**, especially offline, from delayed past STFT context. WPE is still attractive because it is batch-oriented, interpretable, and mathematically grounded. It should absolutely be in your app as a speech-focused dereverb baseline and as a **preconditioner** before heavier ML restoration. citeturn37view0turn22view6turn35view0

Clicks, pops, crackle, and tiny waveform discontinuities are exactly the place where pure DSP still shines. Oudre’s IPOL method treats impulsive corruption as a two-step problem—**detection** and **interpolation**—under the assumption that the local clean signal can be modeled as an autoregressive process. RX’s own module design points the same way: its Interpolate module is specifically for repairing short clicks, and its Mouth De-click is specialized for lip noises and vocal mouth artifacts. That makes the V1 design choice straightforward: use classical detection plus AR/LPC inpainting for clicks and repair transients surgically, instead of trying to make a large model hallucinate across every microscopic defect. citeturn22view5turn40view0

Declipping sits in the middle between classic inverse problems and ML. APPLADE is a useful signal here because it explicitly combines **sparse optimization** with a learned denoiser in a plug-and-play optimization loop, and the authors report better robustness to train/test mismatch than plain DNN declippers. If you want your product to feel “premium” rather than merely “AI”, this is exactly the kind of hybrid thinking worth copying: constrained signal reconstruction, but improved by learned priors where the inverse problem is underdetermined. citeturn28view0turn28view2

## Offline ML and open-source building blocks

For **speech and voiceover**, the practical open-source stack is already rich. **DeepFilterNet** is a low-complexity full-band 48 kHz speech enhancement framework and remains attractive as a lightweight local denoiser or residual-cleanup model. **ClearerVoice-Studio** is broader and more ambitious: it packages speech enhancement, speech separation, super-resolution, target speaker extraction, and metric tooling, and explicitly supports 48 kHz workflows and noisy-reverberant training/inference setups. **Resemble Enhance** adds an easy-to-understand two-stage structure—denoiser plus enhancer—with speech denoising and bandwidth extension on high-rate speech. **VoiceFixer** and **VoiceRestore** are more restoration-oriented: VoiceFixer targets noise, reverberation, bandwidth loss, and clipping in one model, while VoiceRestore uses flow-matching transformers for broader quality restoration of degraded recordings. All of those are downloadable, locally runnable building blocks rather than cloud APIs. citeturn20view0turn21view5turn20view1turn20view2turn22view0

For **speech dereverb**, the most useful split is between **production-ready classical** and **frontier research**. WPE belongs in the first bucket. **BUDDy** belongs in the second: it combines a likelihood term with a diffusion prior and jointly estimates the room filter, which makes it a strong reference for what “offline high-quality dereverb” can look like when runtime is allowed to be slower. I would not make BUDDy your only dereverb path in a commercial V1, but I would absolutely prototype against it, especially for “HQ Render” mode. citeturn22view6turn18view5

For **singing and mixed-vocal material**, you should think in two layers: **enhancement** and **source isolation**. On the enhancement side, **MBTFNet** is a direct singing-voice-enhancement paper and is more aligned with your use case than generic speech enhancers. **AnyEnhance** is the most interesting 2025-era research direction because it explicitly unifies speech and singing enhancement and supports denoising, dereverberation, declipping, super-resolution, and target-speaker extraction without task-specific fine-tuning. The tradeoff is maturity: the paper is strong, and I found public evidence of model releases and challenge baselines, but it is still closer to a research frontier than to a battle-tested desktop inference SDK. citeturn33view0turn21view0turn21view1turn39search0turn39search5

On the source-isolation side, **Demucs** still matters a lot in practice. Hybrid Transformer Demucs explicitly combines waveform and spectrogram domains and reported a measurable SDR gain plus better subjective quality over earlier non-hybrid versions. The official Demucs repo remains a core reference, and **demucs.cpp** is strategically important for you because it shows a realistic path to **C++ inference** for the Demucs family without shipping a Python runtime. **Ultimate Vocal Remover** matters less as a library than as proof that users already value local desktop wrappers around state-of-the-art separation models. This is useful even if your app is not a “vocal remover”: for singing restoration, internal stem extraction can be a **preprocessing step** that makes the later denoiser/dereverberator work dramatically better. citeturn25view0turn20view5turn20view3turn20view4

For artificial vocal reverb, the relevant lesson is that **music-production reverb is not just room acoustics**. The 2022 unsupervised vocal dereverb paper argued that artificial reverb is broader and harder to generalize across than natural reverb, and ReverbFX/SingingReverbFX later created benchmarks built specifically around plugin-derived reverberation. That means a “Voice Dereverb” button trained only on room impulse responses may do fine on podcast room tone while failing on lush plate or exaggerated hall effects in sung vocals. A premium app should therefore expose at least two dereverb personalities: **room cleanup** and **musical/artificial reverb reduction**. citeturn34view0turn22view8turn33view1

One caution for commercialization: not every attractive research dataset is safe for product training. SingingReverbFX/ReverbFX are explicitly released for **non-commercial research**. So use them for internal comparison and benchmarking if helpful, but do not assume you can train a shipping commercial model on that data without clearing rights or replacing it with licensed/synthetic alternatives you own. citeturn33view1

## Product architecture for a premium standalone app

The right product architecture is **file-first, project-based, non-destructive, and spectrogram-native**. Think “restoration workstation,” not “live insert plugin.” RX’s current positioning is revealing here: spectrogram painting, stem views, spectral repair, interpolative click repair, mouth de-click, music rebalance, and assistant-driven analysis all live inside one editor. That is the model to beat. A user should be able to open a take, inspect the spectrogram, apply chain presets, audition deltas, and then surgically override the automation where the model got it wrong. citeturn40view0

A strong V1 render graph would look like this in practice. First, **analysis**: detect sample rate, mono/stereo, clipping, noise profile, likely source type, and whether the file is closer to spoken voice, isolated singing, or vocals in a mix. Second, **localized repair**: click/pop removal, mouth-noise control, and declipping before broader denoise passes so that localized outliers do not confuse later models. Third, **content-aware enhancement**: speech-specific denoise/dereverb for voiceover; singing-specific enhancement for isolated vocals; optional source separation first if the vocal is embedded in music. Fourth, **restoration extras**: bandwidth extension or super-resolution for damaged archival or low-rate material. Fifth, **residual handoff**: spectral repair tools and manual masking for the annoying last five percent. That sequence is an engineering inference, but it follows directly from the strengths and limits documented across DSP restoration, speech enhancement, singing enhancement, and current restoration products. citeturn22view5turn28view0turn21view5turn33view0turn20view5turn40view0

I would also split the app internally into three quality modes rather than one universal pass. **Natural** should lean on conservative DSP plus lighter ML cleanup. **Balanced** should be your default smart chain. **Restoration HQ** should allow multi-pass rendering and heavier models such as flow-matching or diffusion-style restoration where needed. The reason this is worth building is visible in current research: generative models are strong, but benchmarking on SingVERSE shows a perceptual-quality versus intelligibility trade-off, while URGENT-style evaluation distinguishes tasks where regression is enough from tasks that require generation. Exposing modes is not just UX polish; it is honest signal processing. citeturn21view2turn27view0

For internal QA, do not rely on one score. URGENT evaluates with intrusive metrics like PESQ, STOI, SDR, and MCD, non-intrusive metrics like DNSMOS and NISQA, plus downstream compatibility metrics. ClearerVoice’s SpeechScore toolkit bundles many of the same families, including PESQ, STOI, DNSMOS, and SI-SDR. For your app, that suggests a practical evaluation stack: objective metrics for regression testing, plus human listening panels for the final decision-maker, especially on vocal naturalness and reverb tails. citeturn24view0turn21view5

One more product-level recommendation: include a **difference monitor** and **reblend controls** everywhere. Waves Clarity Vx Pro’s workflow emphasizes voice-versus-ambience control; Accentize dxSplit is literally sold around independent control of voice, reverb, and noise; RX increasingly exposes layer and stem rebalancing. Users trust restoration tools more when they can hear what was removed and back some of it off. For a premium app, that is not optional. citeturn18view10turn18view9turn40view0

## Tech stack options and the later DAW path

The best first-choice stack for your goal is **C++20 core + JUCE shell + ONNX Runtime + FFTW + Eigen + libsndfile + SIMD acceleration**. JUCE is still the most pragmatic cross-platform audio framework if you care about both standalone desktop apps and a future plugin path; it supports standalone apps across desktop platforms and the major plugin formats, and it integrates with CMake. ONNX Runtime gives you a production-oriented edge inference engine for local ML. FFTW is still a strong DFT workhorse, Eigen is a reliable linear-algebra backbone, libsndfile handles broad file I/O, and xsimd gives you portable SIMD acceleration when you want hand-optimized CPU kernels without writing raw intrinsics for every architecture. citeturn20view8turn20view10turn20view11turn20view12turn12search7turn20view13

That stack is especially attractive because it supports your most likely end state: a **native desktop restoration editor** with optional local model inference and a later plugin bridge. It also minimizes language-boundary pain when you need very fast custom DSP, custom memory layout for STFT blocks, and deterministic offline rendering. If I had to pick one stack to maximize both product quality and long-term leverage, this would be it. The evidence is indirect rather than absolute, but the ecosystem fit is strong: JUCE owns the audio-app/plugin lane, ONNX Runtime owns local model serving better than ad hoc Python embeddings in a shipping app, and demucs.cpp proves that even music-separation inference can be pulled into a C++ distribution story when necessary. citeturn20view8turn20view10turn20view3

A second strong option is **C++ core + Qt UI + JUCE or iPlug2 for later plugin wrapping**. Qt is stronger than JUCE for large, sophisticated desktop interfaces, data views, custom editors, and general application UX. If you picture this app becoming a serious editor with asset browsers, render queues, history graphs, spectrogram workspaces, and deep compare tools, Qt is compelling. Then you can keep the actual DSP/ML engine as a library and wrap that library later with JUCE or iPlug2 for VST3/CLAP/AU targets. iPlug2 is explicitly designed for cross-platform audio plug-ins and apps with a minimalistic audio/GUI abstraction. citeturn20view9turn20view16turn20view8

The third option is **Rust core + Tauri frontend + CPAL + native ML/DSP bindings**. Tauri is attractive if you want small binaries, modern app tooling, and a web-tech UI over a Rust backend, while CPAL gives you cross-platform audio I/O. This stack is viable for a polished standalone utility, especially if distribution ergonomics and frontend iteration speed matter. I would still rank it below the C++ options for your specific product because the pro-audio plugin ecosystem, sample-accurate edge cases, and reuse of existing audio-restoration code all still lean C++-first. So I see Rust/Tauri as a legitimate option, but more as an optimization for app-platform engineering than as the shortest path to a flagship audio-restoration engine. citeturn20view14turn20view15

For later DAW integration, I would not start with “insert plugin first.” Start standalone. Then, when the core engine is good enough, add **VST3** and possibly **CLAP** for broad host compatibility. The VST3 SDK is now MIT-licensed, which reduces friction for commercial adoption, and CLAP provides a modern open audio-plugin ABI. But if you eventually want deep clip-based, non-realtime integration—the kind of workflow that suits restoration much better than a live insert—**ARA is the strategically important path**. ARA allows plugins to read audio from the DAW host at will and to exchange broader content information rather than living inside realtime buffer constraints. For a restoration tool, that is a better long-term fit than pretending everything is a channel-strip insert. citeturn20view6turn20view7turn18view11

So the clean recommendation is this. **Build V1 as a standalone desktop restoration workstation.** Use **C++20 + JUCE + ONNX Runtime** unless you decide the UI must be even richer than JUCE comfortably supports, in which case use **C++ core + Qt** and wrap the engine later. Make the engine hybrid: **DSP first for precision, local ML where it clearly wins**. Treat **voiceover, isolated singing, and vocals in a mix as separate operating modes**. Then, only after quality is genuinely excellent, decide whether the DAW path should be **VST3/CLAP for convenience** or **ARA for serious workflow integration**. citeturn20view8turn20view10turn21view5turn33view0turn18view11
